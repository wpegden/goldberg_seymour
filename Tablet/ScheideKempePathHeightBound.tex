\begin{lemma}[Prescribed Kempe path height bound]
Let $(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be a globally maximum $k$-triple, with first two
vertices $y_0,y_1$.  If
$\alpha\in\overline\varphi(y_0)$ and
$\beta\in\overline\varphi(y_1)$, then the full
$(\alpha,\beta)$-component through $y_0$ has a simple incidence path $P$
from $y_0$ to $y_1$.  Every vertex-node of $P$ belongs to $V(T)$, its
incidence length is divisible by four, and
\[
 \frac{\operatorname{length}(P)}2+1\leq h(G).
\]
Here the quotient converts incidence length to the number of multigraph
edge identities on the path; the additional one converts edge count to
multigraph vertex count.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $y_0=T.vertex(0)$, $y_1=T.vertex(1)$, and
$X=V(T)$, where the last notation has the meaning in
\noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet}.  We first derive the fixed-pair
maximality needed below.  By \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}, the hypotheses
give all three assertions
\[
 (G,e_0,\varphi)\text{ is a $k$-triple},\qquad
 T\text{ is a Tashkinov tree},\qquad |X|=t(G).                \tag{1}
\]
Let $S$ be any Tashkinov tree for the same $e_0$ and $\varphi$ such that
$X\subseteq V(S)$.  The equality $\chi'(G)=k+1$ in
\noderef{KTriple} says that the integer called $k$ here is exactly
$\chi'(G)-1$.  Hence $S$, together with this same edge and coloring, is
one of the candidates in the maximum defining \noderef{TashkinovOrder}.
It follows that $|V(S)|\leq t(G)$.  On the other hand, the inclusion and
(1) give $t(G)=|X|\leq |V(S)|$.  Thus $|V(S)|=|X|$, and equality of the
cardinalities of these finite sets together with $X\subseteq V(S)$ gives
$V(S)=X$, in particular $V(S)\subseteq X$.  This is every clause of
\noderef{FixedPairMaximalTashkinovTree}: the first clause is the
Tashkinov-tree assertion in (1), and the implication just proved is its
maximality clause.  Therefore
\[
 T\text{ is fixed-pair maximal for }(e_0,\varphi).                \tag{2}
\]

Apply \noderef{ScheideMaximalKempePath} with its two tree arguments both
equal to $T$, with indices $0<1$, and with the prescribed colors
$\alpha,\beta$.  The hypotheses on the missing colors agree literally
with those in that lemma.  We obtain $\alpha\ne\beta$ and an incidence
path $P$ from the vertex-node $y_0$ to the vertex-node $y_1$ such that
\[
 \begin{split}
 &P\text{ is simple},\qquad
 \operatorname{supp}(P)=
   \operatorname{Comp}_{\alpha,\beta}(y_0),\qquad
 4\mid\operatorname{length}(P),                                   \tag{3}\\
 &\mathrm{inl}(v)\in\operatorname{supp}(P)
      \Longrightarrow v\in X.                                    \tag{4}
 \end{split}
\]
Here the component is exactly \noderef{TwoColorComponent}; thus (3)--(4)
already give the component, divisibility, and containment conclusions
required in the statement.  It remains only to prove the height bound for
this same $P$.

Unfold \noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}.  Its adjacencies always join a
vertex-node to an edge-node, so $P$ alternates between the two sides.
Because it starts and ends on the vertex side, write
\[
 P=(\mathrm{inl}(a_0),\mathrm{inr}(f_1),\mathrm{inl}(a_1),
       \ldots,\mathrm{inr}(f_m),\mathrm{inl}(a_m)),                \tag{5}
\]
where $a_0=y_0$, $a_m=y_1$, and
\[
 2m=\operatorname{length}(P),\qquad
 m=\operatorname{length}(P)/2.                                   \tag{6}
\]
The path is simple, so the vertices $a_0,\ldots,a_m$ are pairwise
distinct and the individual edge identities $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ are
pairwise distinct.  This conclusion concerns the nodes
$\mathrm{inr}(f_r)$ and therefore does not identify parallel edge
identities that happen to have equal endpoints.

For later use we now determine the endpoints and color of every one of
these identities.  The two consecutive adjacencies at
$\mathrm{inr}(f_r)$ in (5), when expanded using
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}, give
\[
 \noderef{Incident}(G,a_{r-1},f_r),\qquad
 \noderef{Incident}(G,a_r,f_r),qquad
 \varphi(f_r)=\alpha\ \hbox{or}\ \varphi(f_r)=\beta.             \tag{7}
\]
We stress the endpoint conclusion encoded by the first two assertions.
By \noderef{Incident}, the first assertion lets us write the unordered
endpoint pair as $G.\operatorname{ends}(f_r)=\{a_{r-1},w\}$ for some
$w$.  The loopless clause of \noderef{LooplessMultigraph} gives
$w\ne a_{r-1}$.  The second incidence assertion says that $a_r$ is one of
these two endpoints.  Simplicity of $P$ gives
$a_r\ne a_{r-1}$, so $a_r=w$.  Consequently, for every
$1\leq r\leq m$,
\[
       G.\operatorname{ends}(f_r)=\{a_{r-1},a_r\}.                \tag{8}
\]
This derives the exact unordered endpoint equality from the two incidence
adjacencies for each actual identity $f_r$.

The first two vertices of the original tree are distinct by the vertex
injectivity clause of \noderef{TreeSequence}; hence $y_0\ne y_1$, and
$P$ has positive length.  A positive integer divisible by four is at
least four, so (3) and (6) give
\[
                         m\geq2.                                 \tag{9}
\]
Moreover none of the $f_r$ equals $e_0$.  Indeed, (7) says that every
$f_r$ is colored.  The $k$-triple assertion in (1), unfolded through
\noderef{KTriple} and \noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored}, says that $e_0$ is
uncolored.  Therefore
\[
                       f_r\ne e_0\quad(1\leq r\leq m).            \tag{10}
\]

We next construct the reversed-path initial tree without suppressing any
of its indices.  Define
\[
 U=(b_0,g_0,b_1,\ldots,g_{m-1},b_m)
\]
in the notation of \noderef{TreeSequence} by using the following Lean
zero-based vertex and edge maps:
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c@{\qquad}c|c}
 j&b_j&j&g_j\\ \hline
 0&a_0=y_0&0&e_0\\
 1&a_m=y_1&1\leq j\leq m-1&f_{m+1-j}\\
 2\leq j\leq m&a_{m+1-j}&&
 \end{array}                                                       \tag{11}
\]
Thus, in the paper's alternating notation,
\[
 U=(y_0,e_0,y_1,f_m,a_{m-1},f_{m-1},a_{m-2},
          \ldots,f_2,a_1),                                       \tag{12}
\]
and $U$ has length $m$.

We verify every tree-sequence clause in \noderef{TreeSequence}.  Its
length is positive by (9).  The displayed vertex map is a permutation of
$a_0,\ldots,a_m$, so it is injective by simplicity of $P$.  The displayed
edge map is injective by pairwise distinctness of the $f_r$ and (10).
Its first edge is $e_0$ by definition.  For the first attachment, apply
the attachment clause of the original $T$ at its zero-based edge index
$0$.  Its only earlier vertex index is $0$, while its new vertex index is
$1$, and its first-edge clause identifies that edge with $e_0$; hence
\[
             G.\operatorname{ends}(e_0)=\{y_1,y_0\}
                   =\{b_1,b_0\}.                                \tag{13}
\]
For a remaining edge index $1\leq j\leq m-1$, put
$r=m+1-j$, so $2\leq r\leq m$.  Equations (8) and (11) give
\[
 G.\operatorname{ends}(g_j)
   =G.\operatorname{ends}(f_r)
   =\{a_{r-1},a_r\}=\{b_{j+1},b_j\}.                             \tag{14}
\]
Thus $g_j$ attaches the new vertex $b_{j+1}$ to the immediately preceding
vertex $b_j$, which is an allowed earlier vertex.  Equations (13)--(14)
prove all attachment clauses, so $U$ is a TreeSequence, with the actual
edge identities retained throughout.

We also verify the Tashkinov condition of \noderef{TashkinovTree} for
$U$.  There is nothing to prove for the distinguished edge at zero-based
index $0$.  Let $1\leq j\leq m-1$.  By (7), the edge
$g_j=f_{m+1-j}$ has color $\alpha$ or color $\beta$.  In the first case,
the given $\alpha\in\overline\varphi(y_0)$, interpreted by
\noderef{MissingColors}, supplies the earlier witness $b_0=y_0$.  In the
second case, the given $\beta\in\overline\varphi(y_1)$ supplies the
earlier witness $b_1=y_1$.  Both indices $0$ and $1$ precede the newly
adjoined vertex index $j+1$ because $j\geq1$.  Hence every required edge
has an earlier missing-color witness.  Notice that this argument uses
only the disjunction in (7); it neither assumes nor needs alternation of
the colors along $P$.  We have proved
\[
                    U\text{ is a Tashkinov tree}.                 \tag{15}
\]

Apply \noderef{ScheideMaximalExtendsEveryTree} to the $k$-triple in (1),
the fixed-pair maximal tree in (2), and the Tashkinov tree (15).  It gives
a Tashkinov tree $T'$ such that
\[
 V(T')=X,\qquad U\text{ is an ordered initial segment of }T'.    \tag{16}
\]
By \noderef{TreeSequenceInitialSegment}, the second assertion means
precisely that
\[
 m=U.\operatorname{length}\leq T'.\operatorname{length}          \tag{17}
\]
and that $T'.vertex(j)=b_j$ for $0\leq j\leq m$ and
\[
 T'.edge(j)=g_j\quad\text{for }0\leq j<m.                        \tag{18}
\]
The same $k$-triple is still in force, $T'$ is Tashkinov by construction,
and (1) and (16) give $|V(T')|=|X|=t(G)$.  These are exactly the three
clauses of \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}, so
\[
                   (T',e_0,\varphi)\text{ is globally maximum}.  \tag{19}
\]

We now instantiate \noderef{NormalGlobalMaximumTriple} field by field.
Choose height
\[
                    p=m+1                                      \tag{20}
\]
and choose as its last index the index $m$ of $T'$.  This is a legal index
by (17), it satisfies $m+1=p$, and (9) gives $2\leq p$, while (17) gives
$p\leq T'.\operatorname{length}+1$.  Use the same $\alpha,\beta$ as the
two colors.  Equations (18) at indices $0$ and $1$ identify the first two
vertices of $T'$ with $y_0,y_1$, so the two prescribed missing-color
hypotheses give exactly the `alpha_missing' and `beta_missing' fields.
For the closing edge choose the individual identity $f_1$.  At index $m$,
(11) and (18) give $T'.vertex(m)=b_m=a_1$, while
$T'.vertex(0)=a_0$; hence (8) for $r=1$ is precisely
\[
 G.\operatorname{ends}(f_1)
    =\{T'.vertex(m),T'.vertex(0)\},                               \tag{21}
\]
with the order immaterial in the unordered pair.  This proves the exact
closing-end field.

Take the reversal of $P$ as the stored closing walk.  It starts at
$a_m=y_1=T'.vertex(1)$ and ends at
$a_0=y_0=T'.vertex(0)$.  Reversal preserves simplicity, support, and
length, so (3), (6), and (20) give
\[
 \operatorname{length}(P^{\mathrm{rev}})=2m=2(p-1),              \tag{22}
\]
which proves the closing-walk endpoint, path, and length fields.

It remains to check both support fields, rather than infer them merely
from the word ``simple.''  The alternating display (5) shows that the
vertex-nodes in the support of $P^{\mathrm{rev}}$ are exactly
\[
 \mathrm{inl}(a_0),\mathrm{inl}(a_1),\ldots,
 \mathrm{inl}(a_m).                                               \tag{23}
\]
The first $p=m+1$ vertices $b_0,\ldots,b_m$ in (11) are exactly these
same vertices in the order
$a_0,a_m,a_{m-1},\ldots,a_1$, and (18) transports them to the first
$p$ vertices of $T'$.  Therefore, for every multigraph vertex $v$,
\[
 \mathrm{inl}(v)\in\operatorname{supp}(P^{\mathrm{rev}})
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{there is an index }0\leq i<p\text{ with }v=T'.vertex(i).  \tag{24}
\]
This is exactly `closingPath_vertices'.

Similarly, (5) says that the edge-nodes in the reversed support are
exactly the individual nodes
\[
             \mathrm{inr}(f_1),\ldots,\mathrm{inr}(f_m).          \tag{25}
\]
The first is the chosen closing edge.  For $2\leq r\leq m$, set
$j=m+1-r$.  Then $1\leq j\leq m-1$, so
$1\leq j$ and $j+2\leq m+1=p$, and (11), (18) give
$f_r=g_j=T'.edge(j)$.  Conversely, every index satisfying
$1\leq j$ and $j+2\leq p=m+1$ lies in $1\leq j\leq m-1$ and yields
$r=m+1-j$ in the range $2\leq r\leq m$.  Thus, for every edge identity
$f$,
\[
 \mathrm{inr}(f)\in\operatorname{supp}(P^{\mathrm{rev}})
 \Longleftrightarrow
 f=f_1\ \hbox{or}\
 \bigl(\exists j,\ 1\leq j,\ j+2\leq p,\ f=T'.edge(j)\bigr).     \tag{26}
\]
This is exactly `closingPath_edges', including its two index bounds.

Finally take any zero-based edge index $j$ with
$1\leq j$ and $j+2\leq p$.  Then $1\leq j\leq m-1$, and with the explicit
reindexing $r=m+1-j$ equations (8), (11), and (18) give
\[
 \begin{split}
 G.\operatorname{ends}(T'.edge(j))
 &=G.\operatorname{ends}(f_r)
   =\{a_{r-1},a_r\}\\
 &=\{b_{j+1},b_j\}
   =\{T'.vertex(j),T'.vertex(j+1)\}.
 \end{split}                                                       \tag{27}
\]
The last equality only swaps the entries of an unordered pair.  Since
$j$ was arbitrary, (27) proves every `trunk_edge_ends' equation.  Together
with (19)--(26), it completes every field of a normal globally maximum
witness of chosen height $p=m+1$.

It remains only to place this witness in the finite maximum defining
\noderef{ScheideGraphHeight}.  The height bound already proved as a normal
field gives $p\leq T'.\operatorname{length}+1$.  The vertex map of the
TreeSequence $T'$ is injective into the finite vertex type of $G$, so
$T'.\operatorname{length}+1\leq |V(G)|$.  Hence $p$ lies in the defining
range, and the normal witness just constructed puts $p$ in the set of
heights over which $h(G)$ is the maximum.  Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{length}(P)/2+1=m+1=p\leq h(G).                    \tag{28}
\]
Choosing the original $P$ from (3), assertions (3)--(4) and (28) are all
the conclusions of the lemma.
\end{proof}
