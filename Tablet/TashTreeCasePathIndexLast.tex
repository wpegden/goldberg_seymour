\begin{lemma}[Minimum bad pair: last path-index case]
Let \((\varphi,T)\) be a minimum bad pair with \(\rho(T)>2\).  The case
\(\rho(T)=|E(T)|\) is impossible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write \(T=(y_0,e_1,y_1,\ldots,e_n,y_n)\) and \(q=\rho(T)=n\).  The last
edge \(e_n\) does not join \(y_{n-1}\) to \(y_n\), since otherwise the
suffix path would begin at \(n-1\), contrary to the minimality of the path
index in \noderef{TashTreePathIndex}.  We successively establish three
normal forms.  Every recoloring used below preserves a minimum bad pair by
\noderef{TashTreeControlledInterchange}, so the proper-prefix
elementarity and unused-color count remain applicable.

First we arrange that a color \(\beta\) is missing at \(y_n\) and at some
\(y_j\), \(j\le n-2\), and is unused on every displayed colored tree
edge.  Since the last proper prefix is elementary but \(T\) is bad, begin
with a color \(\beta\) missing at \(y_n\) and some \(y_j\), \(j<n\).
By \noderef{TashTreeFourUnusedColors}, the prefix \(T_{n-2}\) has at least
four missing colors unused on its colored edges.  The two further exact
edges \(e_{n-1},e_n\) use at most two colors.  Hence at least two of those
four colors are unused on all of \(T\); choose one, \(\alpha\ne\beta\),
missing at a vertex \(y_m\), \(m\le n-2\).

If \(j=n-1\), then \(\beta\) is unused on \(T_{n-1}\): any use there
would have an earlier missing-\(\beta\) witness by the Tashkinov condition,
contradicting elementariness of that prefix.  It is not used on \(e_n\),
which is incident with the \(\beta\)-missing vertex \(y_n\).  Thus the
controlled \((\alpha,\beta)\)-interchange between \(y_m\) and \(y_{n-1}\)
moves the earlier missing occurrence of \(\beta\) to \(y_m\), while the
third-endpoint argument in \noderef{TashTreeControlledInterchange} leaves
it missing at \(y_n\).  This gives the claimed normal form.

Suppose instead that \(j\le n-2\).  If \(\beta\) is already unused on the
whole tree, the first normal form holds.  Otherwise follow the maximal
\((\alpha,\beta)\)-chain \(Q\) starting at \(y_n\).  If \(Q\) meets
\(V(T_{n-2})\), traverse it from \(y_n\), let \(y_h\) be its first vertex
in that prefix, and append the intervening alternating segment to
\(T_{n-2}\).  Its internal vertices are outside the retained prefix.  Each
appended edge has color \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\), and these colors have the
earlier missing witnesses \(y_m,y_j\) in the retained prefix.  Thus the
result is a Tashkinov tree.  It is bad because \(\beta\) is still missing
at \(y_j,y_n\), while its appended suffix is a path and its path index is
at most \(n-1<q\).  This contradicts the minimality clause of
\noderef{TashTreeMinimumBadPair}.

Consequently \(Q\) avoids \(T_{n-2}\).  Every displayed tree edge has an
endpoint in this prefix: this is immediate for \(e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1}\),
and for \(e_n\) it follows from the already-proved fact that its earlier
endpoint is not \(y_{n-1}\).  Hence \(Q\) contains no tree-edge identity.
Swap \(\alpha,\beta\) on \(Q\), using
\noderef{TwoColorComponentSwapProper}, and then globally transpose the
names \(\alpha,\beta\).  The local swap fixes every tree edge and every
needed earlier witness; the global transposition preserves properness and
all witness relations.  In the resulting coloring the color now called
\(\beta\) is missing at \(y_m,y_n\), and any tree edge colored \(\beta\)
would previously have been colored \(\alpha\), contrary to the choice of
\(\alpha\).  The first normal form follows.

Second, put \(\gamma=\varphi(e_n)\) and arrange that \(\gamma\) is present
at \(y_{n-1}\).  If it is missing there, elementariness of \(T_{n-1}\)
and the Tashkinov witness for \(e_n\) show that \(y_{n-1}\) is the unique
vertex in that prefix missing \(\gamma\).  It follows, as above, that
\(\gamma\) is unused through \(T_{n-1}\).  The color \(\beta\) from the
first normal form is unused everywhere, so apply a controlled
\((\beta,\gamma)\)-interchange between its earlier missing vertex and
\(y_{n-1}\).  Its component cannot contain \(y_n\): the vertices at its
two controlled ends have degree at most one, while \(y_n\), which misses
\(\beta\) and is incident with the \(\gamma\)-colored \(e_n\), would be
a third end.  Thus \(e_n\) keeps color \(\gamma\), while \(\gamma\)
becomes present at \(y_{n-1}\).

Third, arrange that some color is missing at \(y_n\) and at a vertex of
\(T_{n-2}\).  Choose a repeated missing color \(\beta'\) at
\(y_{j'},y_n\).  This is immediate if \(j'\le n-2\).  If \(j'=n-1\),
then prefix elementariness and incidence with \(y_n\) make \(\beta'\)
unused on all of \(T\).  The first normal-form count supplies a distinct
color \(\alpha'\), missing on \(T_{n-2}\) and unused on all of \(T\).
A controlled interchange moves the earlier missing \(\beta'\) into
\(T_{n-2}\) and leaves it missing at \(y_n\).  Neither affected color is
\(\gamma\): that color is used on \(e_n\) and, by the second normal form,
is present at \(y_{n-1}\).  Hence the second normal form survives.

Delete the exact pair \(e_{n-1},y_{n-1}\) from the display and retain
\(e_n,y_n\) as the last pair.  Because \(q=n\), the earlier endpoint of
\(e_n\) lies in \(T_{n-2}\), so the resulting display is a tree-sequence.
The second normal form says that the missing-\(\gamma\) witness for
\(e_n\) is not \(y_{n-1}\), and therefore remains in \(T_{n-2}\); all
other witnesses are inherited.  The third normal form makes the shortened
tree bad.  It has only \(n-1\) edges and path index at most \(n-1<n=q\),
contradicting minimum badness.  Thus this case is impossible.
\end{proof}
