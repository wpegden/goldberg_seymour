\begin{lemma}[Balanced trunk rotation]
Let $(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be balanced of height $p=h(G)$.  For every trunk
index $1\le j\le p-1$, moving the uncolored status around the closing cycle
from $f_0=e_0$ to $f_j$ and rotating the trunk from $y_0$ to $y_j$
produces another balanced globally maximum triple.  The resulting data
satisfy the exact rotation relation \noderef{TrunkRotation}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $p=N.height$, $n=T.length$, and write
$y_r=T.vertex(r)$ for $0\leq r\leq n$.  For $0\leq i<p$, put
\[
             f_i=\noderef{NormalCycleEdge}(N,i).
                                                               \tag{1}
\]
Thus $f_0=e_0$, $f_i=T.edge(i)$ for $1\leq i\leq p-2$, and
$f_{p-1}=N.closingEdge$.  Moreover, $f_i$ has the exact unordered
endpoint pair $\{y_i,y_{i+1}\}$ for $i<p-1$, while $f_{p-1}$ has
endpoint pair $\{y_{p-1},y_0\}$.  These endpoint assertions follow from
\noderef{NormalCycleEdge}, the `trunk_edge_ends' and `closing_ends'
fields of \noderef{NormalGlobalMaximumTriple}; they refer to individual
edge identities, not merely endpoint pairs.

We first recover all information about this closing cycle.  The global
field of $N$, expanded by \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}, says that
$(G,e_0,\varphi)$ is a $k$-triple, $T$ is a Tashkinov tree, and
\[
                         |V(T)|=t(G).                            \tag{2}
\]
By \noderef{KTriple}, $\varphi$ is proper and leaves exactly $e_0$
uncolored in the respective senses of
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring} and
\noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored}.  The balanced hypothesis, unfolded using
\noderef{BalancedGlobalMaximumTriple}, gives $p=h(G)$.  Since (2)
exhibits a globally maximum triple, \noderef{ScheideGraphHeightOdd}
therefore gives
\[
                         p\geq3\quad\hbox{and}\quad p\text{ odd}. \tag{3}
\]

The stored closing walk in \noderef{NormalGlobalMaximumTriple} is a
simple incidence path of length $2(p-1)$.  A simple path of that length
has $2p-1$ distinct nodes.  Its vertex-node support consists of the $p$
nodes $\mathrm{inl}(y_0),\ldots,\mathrm{inl}(y_{p-1})$, which are
distinct by the vertex-injectivity clause of
\noderef{TreeSequence}.  Its edge-node support consists precisely of
\[
       \mathrm{inr}(f_1),\ldots,\mathrm{inr}(f_{p-1})             \tag{4}
\]
by the exact `closingPath_edges' field.  The two sides of the incidence
graph are disjoint, so (4) contains $p-1$ distinct edge-nodes.  Hence the
identities $f_1,\ldots,f_{p-1}$ are pairwise distinct.  Each of them is
colored $\alpha=N.alpha$ or $\beta=N.beta$, because every adjacency of
an edge-node in \noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} records both incidence
and one of those two exact colors.  Since $f_0=e_0$ is uncolored, it is
distinct from every identity in (4).  We have proved that
\[
                  f_0,\ldots,f_{p-1}\text{ are pairwise distinct}. \tag{5}
\]

The endpoint equations in (1), together with $p\geq3$, force the simple
closing path to have the order
\[
 y_1,f_1,y_2,f_2,\ldots,y_{p-1},f_{p-1},y_0.                    \tag{6}
\]
Indeed, among the identities in (4), only $f_1$ is incident with $y_1$;
after traversing it, only $f_2$ is the unused identity incident with
$y_2$, and induction gives the displayed order through $f_{p-1}$.
Here incidence has its literal endpoint meaning from
\noderef{Incident}, and the distinct vertices used in this induction
come from \noderef{TreeSequence}.  Expanding
\noderef{MissingColors} and \noderef{PresentColors}, the fact that
$\beta$ is missing at $y_1$ excludes color $\beta$ from the incident
edge $f_1$, so
\[
                         \varphi(f_1)=\alpha.                    \tag{7}
\]
Likewise $\alpha$ is missing at $y_0$, so
$\varphi(f_{p-1})=\beta$.  In particular $\alpha\ne\beta$: (7) makes
$\alpha$ present at $y_1$, whereas $\beta$ is missing there.  Consecutive
identities in (6) are distinct by (5), share the displayed vertex and
are therefore adjacent by \noderef{AdjacentEdges}; properness from
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring} forces their colors to differ.  Since
only $\alpha$ and $\beta$ occur, the colors alternate.  Thus, for
$1\leq i<p$,
\[
 \varphi(f_i)=
 \begin{cases}
  \alpha,&i\text{ odd},\\
  \beta,&i\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}                                                     \tag{8}
\]
The last value agrees with (3), since $p-1$ is even.

Fix the supplied index $j$, so $1\leq j<p$.  Define a partial coloring
$\psi$ identity by identity as follows:
\[
 \psi(f_j)=\mathrm{uncolored},\qquad
 \psi(f_i)=\varphi(f_{i+1})\ (0\leq i<j),\qquad
 \psi(g)=\varphi(g)\quad
   \bigl(g\notin\{f_0,\ldots,f_j\}\bigr).                       \tag{9}
\]
Pairwise distinctness (5) makes these cases disjoint and makes $\psi$
well-defined.  Every $f_i$ with $i<j$ receives a color, because
$i+1\geq1$ and (8) applies.  If $g\ne f_j$ is outside the shifted set,
then $g\ne e_0=f_0$, so the exact-one-uncolored clause of
\noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored} supplies a color for $g$.  Consequently
\[
                    \psi\text{ leaves exactly }f_j\text{ uncolored}. \tag{10}
\]

We next prove properness rather than infer it from the word ``shift.''
For $0\leq i<j$, let $c_i$ be the old color of $f_{i+1}$, which is the
new color of $f_i$.  At the endpoint $y_{i+1}$, the old identity
$f_{i+1}$ was a $c_i$-colored carrier.  Old properness makes it the unique
$c_i$-colored identity incident there: any distinct second carrier would
share $y_{i+1}$, hence would be adjacent by
\noderef{AdjacentEdges}, contradicting
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring}.  Under (9), this unique old carrier is
either recolored simultaneously, when $i+1<j$, or is the newly uncolored
$f_j$, when $i+1=j$.  Thus no unchanged noncycle edge at $y_{i+1}$ can
conflict with the new color on $f_i$.

At the other endpoint $y_i$ there are three exhaustive cases.  If $i=0$,
then $c_i=\alpha$ by (7), and $\alpha$ was missing at $y_0$; the
definitions \noderef{MissingColors} and \noderef{PresentColors} exclude
every unchanged $\alpha$-colored edge there.  If $i=1$, then (8) gives
$c_i=\beta$, which was missing at $y_1$, so the same argument excludes
every unchanged carrier.  If $2\leq i<j$, alternation (8) gives
\[
                    \varphi(f_{i-1})=c_i.                        \tag{11}
\]
The old carrier $f_{i-1}$ is unique at $y_i$ by old properness.  It too is
recolored in (9), now receiving the different old color of $f_i$.
Therefore no unchanged noncycle identity at $y_i$ conflicts in this case
either.  This checks both endpoints of every shifted identity and also
explains explicitly where its incoming color was missing or which unique
old carrier was recolored at the same time.

By the endpoint equations in (1), vertex injectivity, and $p\geq3$, two
cycle identities share an endpoint only when their indices are consecutive
modulo $p$.  Two consecutive shifted cycle identities receive consecutive
old cycle colors and hence different colors by (8).  At $y_0$, the only
possible additional cycle comparison is between $f_0$ and $f_{p-1}$: the former
gets $\alpha$ and the latter either remains $\beta$ by (8), or, when
$j=p-1$, is uncolored.  At the other end of the shifted segment $f_j$ is
uncolored.  Every pair of colored identities not involving a shifted
identity keeps its old colors and is proper by (2).  The preceding
endpoint analysis covers every remaining pair consisting of one shifted
identity and one unchanged identity.  By the definition
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring}, this proves
\[
                         \psi\text{ is proper}.                  \tag{12}
\]

Let $B$ be the old color of $f_j$, and let $A$ be the other member of
$\{\alpha,\beta\}$.  Equation (8) makes this choice unique and gives
$A\ne B$.  We record the two missing-color facts needed for the rotated
normal data.  At $y_j$, if $j=1$, the color $A=\beta$ was already
missing and (9) adds only color $B=\alpha$ there.  If $j>1$, the unique
old $A$-carrier at $y_j$ was $f_{j-1}$ by (8) and old properness; (9)
recolors that edge with $B$, while $f_j$ becomes uncolored.  In both
cases no other incident identity changes to $A$, so
\[
                          A\in\overline\psi(y_j).                 \tag{13}
\]
At $y_{j+1\pmod p}$, the unique old $B$-carrier was $f_j$.  This follows
from old properness, including the case $j=p-1$, where the endpoint is
$y_0$.  The edge $f_j$ becomes uncolored.  The other cycle edge at this
vertex has new color $A$: it is the unchanged $f_{j+1}$ when $j<p-1$,
and it is $f_0$, with new color $\varphi(f_1)=\alpha=A$, when $j=p-1$
(in the latter case (3) makes $B=\beta$).  No noncycle identity can have
old color $B$ at this vertex, by uniqueness, and none is changed.  Hence
\[
                    B\in\overline\psi(y_{j+1\pmod p}).           \tag{14}
\]
Statements (13)--(14) use exactly the complement relation in
\noderef{MissingColors} and the carrier description in
\noderef{PresentColors}.

We now construct the rotated tree-sequence with all indices exposed.  For
$0\leq r<p$, set
\[
                 \rho(r)=(j+r)\bmod p.                           \tag{15}
\]
Give $T'$ length $n$ and define its zero-based maps by
\[
 T'.vertex(r)=
 \begin{cases}
  y_{\rho(r)},&r<p,\\
  y_r,&p\leq r\leq n,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 T'.edge(r)=
 \begin{cases}
  f_{\rho(r)},&r+1<p,\\
  T.edge(r),&p-1\leq r<n.
 \end{cases}                                                     \tag{16}
\]
The ranges in (16) cover all indices, and (3) makes the common length
positive.

We verify every clause of \noderef{TreeSequence}.  Addition by $j$ modulo
$p$ is a permutation of $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$: if
$\rho(r)=\rho(s)$ for $r,s<p$, cancellation in the cyclic group gives
$r=s$.  Thus the first vertex block in (16) is injective.  It uses exactly
the old indices below $p$, while the tail fixes every index at least $p$.
The old vertex map is injective, so no first-block vertex equals a tail
vertex and the whole new vertex map is injective.

The new trunk edges are the $p-1$ cycle identities other than
\[
        f_{(j+p-1)\bmod p}=f_{j-1};                              \tag{17}
\]
they are pairwise distinct by (5).  The old tail identities
$T.edge(r)$ for $r\geq p-1$ are pairwise distinct by the edge-injectivity
clause of \noderef{TreeSequence}.  They are also disjoint from every
cycle identity.  For $0\leq i\leq p-2$, the definition
\noderef{NormalCycleEdge} identifies $f_i$ with $T.edge(i)$, so old edge
injectivity separates it from $T.edge(r)$ with $r\geq p-1$.  For the
remaining identity $f_{p-1}=N.closingEdge$, suppose it equaled such a
$T.edge(r)$.  The attachment clause of \noderef{TreeSequence} would make
its endpoint pair contain the fresh vertex $y_{r+1}$, whose index is at
least $p$; the `closing_ends' field of
\noderef{NormalGlobalMaximumTriple} makes the same pair
$\{y_{p-1},y_0\}$.  Vertex injectivity says that $y_{r+1}$ is neither of
those two vertices, a contradiction.  This proves both the old closing
edge's distinctness from every tail identity and injectivity of the whole
new edge map.  Formula (16) at $r=0$ and (15) give
\[
                         T'.edge(0)=f_j,                         \tag{18}
\]
so the new first edge is the new distinguished edge.

For a trunk edge index $r$ with $r+1<p$, the exact endpoint equation in
(1) gives
\[
 G.ends(T'.edge(r))
   =\{y_{\rho(r)},y_{(\rho(r)+1)\bmod p}\}
   =\{T'.vertex(r),T'.vertex(r+1)\}.                             \tag{19}
\]
Thus it attaches the new vertex at position $r+1$ to the immediately
preceding vertex at position $r$.  Now let $r\geq p-1$ be a tail edge
index.  The old attachment clause supplies an old index $a\leq r$ such
that
\[
             G.ends(T.edge(r))=\{y_{r+1},y_a\}.                  \tag{20}
\]
If $a<p$, let $s=(a+p-j)\bmod p$.  More explicitly, $s=a-j$ when
$j\leq a$, while $s=a+p-j$ when $a<j$.  In the first case
$j+s=a<p$, and in the second $j+s=a+p$, so in both cases $s<p$ and
$\rho(s)=a$.  Also $s\leq p-1\leq r$.  If $a\geq p$, put $s=a$,
again with $s\leq r$.
In either case (16) gives $T'.vertex(s)=y_a$, while
$T'.vertex(r+1)=y_{r+1}$.  Equations (16) and (20) therefore give the
required attachment to an earlier new vertex.  We have checked positive
length, both injectivity clauses, the first edge, and every exact
attachment, so (16) defines a TreeSequence.

Next we prove every clause of \noderef{TashkinovTree}.  Delete $f_j$ from
the cycle and traverse the remaining identities from $y_{j+1\pmod p}$
to $y_j$.  Equations (8)--(9), including the wrap through the newly
colored $f_0$, show that these identities alternate between $A$ and $B$.
Consequently every new trunk edge at index $1\leq r<p-1$ has color $A$
or $B$.  In the first case, (13) supplies the earlier vertex
$T'.vertex(0)=y_j$ as its missing-color witness.  In the second case,
(14) supplies $T'.vertex(1)=y_{j+1\pmod p}$.  Both positions are no later
than $r$, exactly as required by the zero-based quantifier in
\noderef{TashkinovTree}.

Let instead $r\geq p-1$ be a tail index.  The original Tashkinov
condition from (2) supplies a color $c$ and an old witness position
$a\leq r$ such that
\[
       \varphi(T.edge(r))=c,\qquad c\in\overline\varphi(y_a).   \tag{21}
\]
The tail-cycle disjointness proved above and (9) give
$\psi(T'.edge(r))=\varphi(T.edge(r))=c$.  If $c=A$ or $c=B$, use the
new missing witness (13) or (14), respectively.  These positions are
earlier because (3) and $r\geq p-1$ give $r\geq2$.  If
$c\notin\{A,B\}=\{\alpha,\beta\}$, every shifted identity had an old and
new color in $\{\alpha,\beta\}$, and every other identity is unchanged.
Thus an identity incident with $y_a$ has color $c$ under $\psi$ exactly
when it had color $c$ under $\varphi$.  By
\noderef{PresentColors} and \noderef{MissingColors}, the second assertion
of (21) remains true under $\psi$.  Place $y_a$ in its new earlier
position $s$ using exactly the inverse-index construction following
(20).  This supplies the required witness for every unchanged tail edge.
Hence $T'$ is a Tashkinov tree for $(f_j,\psi)$.

We can now assemble the new global triple.  The criticality,
chromatic-index, and maximum-degree clauses of the old $k$-triple concern
only $G$ and $k$ and are unchanged.  Assertions (10) and (12) give its
one-uncolored and properness clauses for $(f_j,\psi)$.  Thus
\noderef{KTriple} gives a new $k$-triple.  We have just proved the
Tashkinov-tree clause.  Finally, (15)--(16) cyclically permute precisely
the first $p$ old vertices and fix all later vertices, so
\[
        \noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet}(T')
          =\noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet}(T).                   \tag{22}
\]
Equations (2) and (22) give the maximum-order clause.  These are every
clause of \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}, so
\[
                  (T',f_j,\psi)\text{ is globally maximum}.      \tag{23}
\]

It remains to construct, field by field, normal data $N'$ for (23).  Use
height $p$, colors $A,B$, last index $p-1$, and closing identity
\[
                  q'=f_{(j+p-1)\bmod p}=f_{j-1}.                 \tag{24}
\]
The last index is legal because $p\leq n+1$ is the old `height_upper'
field; it satisfies $(p-1)+1=p$.  The old lower bound gives $2\leq p$,
and the new length is $n$, so both height bounds hold.  Assertions
(13)--(14) are exactly the two missing-color fields at the new first and
second vertices.  Equations (1), (15), and (16) say that (24) has ends
\[
                    \{T'.vertex(p-1),T'.vertex(0)\},             \tag{25}
\]
which is the required closing-end field.

For the stored closing walk, take the incidence sequence
\[
 \begin{split}
 R={}&(\mathrm{inl}(T'.vertex(1)),
       \mathrm{inr}(T'.edge(1)),
       \mathrm{inl}(T'.vertex(2)),\ldots,\\
    &\mathrm{inr}(T'.edge(p-2)),
       \mathrm{inl}(T'.vertex(p-1)),
       \mathrm{inr}(q'),
       \mathrm{inl}(T'.vertex(0))).                              \tag{26}
 \end{split}
\]
Here the middle pattern is taken once for each index
$1\leq r\leq p-2$; in particular, when $p=3$ its first and last displayed
terms describe the same single edge rather than two copies.
For every displayed edge-node, (19) or (25) supplies incidence with its
two neighboring vertex-nodes.  The alternation proved before (21) says
that its $\psi$-color is $A$ or $B$.  By the adjacency definition
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}, each consecutive pair in (26) is
therefore adjacent, so $R$ is a walk in the required two-color incidence
graph.

The vertex-nodes in (26) are the $p$ pairwise distinct first-block
vertices of $T'$.  Its edge-nodes are the $p-1$ cycle identities other
than $f_j$; they are pairwise distinct by (5), and the two sum tags keep
every vertex-node distinct from every edge-node.  Thus $R$ is simple.
It contains $p-1$ multigraph edge-nodes and two incidence steps for each,
so
\[
                         R.length=2(p-1).                        \tag{27}
\]
Its vertex-node support is exactly
\[
 \{\mathrm{inl}(T'.vertex(i)):0\leq i<p\},                      \tag{28}
\]
which proves both directions of `closingPath_vertices'.  Its edge-node
support is exactly $q'$ together with
\[
 \{\mathrm{inr}(T'.edge(r)):1\leq r,\ r+2\leq p\}.             \tag{29}
\]
Indeed, the bounds in (29) are precisely $1\leq r\leq p-2$, the
nonclosing edges explicitly displayed in (26); distinctness (5) proves
the converse without identifying any parallel identities.  This is both
directions of `closingPath_edges'.  Finally, for every index satisfying
the bounds in (29), equation (19) is exactly the required
`trunk_edge_ends' equation.  Together, (23), (25)--(29), the two height
bounds, the last-index equation, and (13)--(14) provide every field of
\noderef{NormalGlobalMaximumTriple}.  Denote this normal witness by $N'$.

We now check the complete conjunction in \noderef{TrunkRotation}.  The
first clause is the supplied inequality $1\leq j$.  Choose the new
distinguished identity $e_1=f_j$; equation (1) is its required
`NormalCycleEdge' identity, and (9) gives $\psi(e_1)$ uncolored.  For
every finite index $i<j$, the first two cases of (9) give exactly
\[
                   \psi(f_i)=\varphi(f_{i+1}),                   \tag{30}
\]
including $i=0$.  If an identity differs from every $f_i$ with
$0\leq i\leq j$, the last case of (9) gives pointwise equality of its
colors.  These are the two quantified coloring clauses, with their exact
finite-index casts.

The construction gives $T'.length=n=T.length$ and
$N'.height=p=N.height$.  Equation (22) is the required vertex-set
equality.  If $r<p$, choose the finite index
$i=(j+r)\bmod p$; the elementary remainder bound gives $i<p$, and (15)
is simultaneously the required equality of index values and the required
vertex equality.  If $p\leq r$, choose the old index $s=r$; the second
case of the vertex formula (16) gives both required tail identities.
Likewise, if $r+1<p$, choose $i=(j+r)\bmod p$ and use the first edge
formula in (16); if $p-1\leq r$, choose $s=r$ and use its second edge
formula.  This proves, one by one, all four quantified trunk and tail
formulas.

For the predecessor clause, put
\[
                   pred=(j+p-1)\bmod p.                          \tag{31}
\]
The remainder bound makes this a finite index below $p$.  Since
$1\leq j<p$,
\[
      j+p-1=p+(j-1),\qquad 0\leq j-1<p,
\]
so the modular calculation gives $pred=j-1$.  The choice (24) is therefore
exactly $N'.closingEdge=f_{pred}$.  Equations (30)--(31), (16), (22), and
the displayed length and height equalities are every formula quantified
by \noderef{TrunkRotation}; hence the constructed data satisfy that
relation.

Finally we transfer balance without suppressing the tail indices.  The
height equation $N'.height=p=h(G)$ was already proved.  Let $r$ satisfy
\[
                         p<2r<n+1.                               \tag{32}
\]
Then $2r-2\geq p-1$, so both zero-based edge indices $2r-2$ and $2r-1$
lie in the unchanged tail range of (16).  The tail-cycle disjointness
proved before (18) shows that these identities lie outside every shifted
cycle identity, and (9) therefore gives the two pointwise equalities
\[
 \psi(T'.edge(2r-2))=\varphi(T.edge(2r-2)),\qquad
 \psi(T'.edge(2r-1))=\varphi(T.edge(2r-1)).                      \tag{33}
\]
The old balance clause in
\noderef{BalancedGlobalMaximumTriple}, applied to the same bounds (32),
equates the right sides of (33).  Hence the left sides are equal.  Since
$r$ was arbitrary, this proves every quantified post-trunk equality, and
therefore $N'$ is balanced.  The witnesses $e_1=f_j$, $\psi$, $T'$, and
$N'$ satisfy both conclusions of the lemma.
\end{proof}
