\begin{lemma}[Defective-chain structure, part (e)]
Let $(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be a globally maximum triple for $G$.  Let
$\alpha\in\mathcal D(V(T),\varphi)$ be defective, let
$\gamma\in\mathcal F(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be free and missing at a vertex
$u\in V(T)$, and let $z\notin V(T)$.  Let the supplied incidence walk $P$
from $u$ to $z$ be a full defective-chain path for
$(T,\varphi,\alpha,\gamma,u,z)$.  If the supplied indices $i_1<i_2$ and
vertices $v_1,v_2$ satisfy the first-departure/last-return predicate for
$(T,\varphi,\alpha,\gamma,u,z,P)$, then $V(T)\cup\{v_1\}$ is elementary
with respect to $\varphi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $X=V(T)$.  Unpacking \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}, the hypothesis
gives a $k$-triple $(G,e_0,\varphi)$ and says that $T$ is a Tashkinov tree
for this triple and that $|X|=t(G)$.  In particular, the
\noderef{KTriple} clauses say that $\varphi$ is proper and leaves exactly
$e_0$ uncolored.  By \noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumOrder}, the set $X$ is
elementary and closed.  We prove first that
\[
 \overline\varphi(v_1)\cap\overline\varphi(x)=\varnothing
 \quad\hbox{for every }x\in X. \tag{1}
\]
Suppose to the contrary that a color $\delta$ belongs to both missing
sets, and fix the corresponding $x\in X$.

We first locate the relevant edge identities on $P$.  The predicate
\noderef{ScheideDepartureReturn} says that the vertex-node at $i_1$ is
$v_1\notin X$, every earlier vertex-node of $P$ lies in $X$, there is a
later vertex-node $v_2\in X$, and $P$ satisfies
\noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainPath}.  The latter predicate says that $P$
is simple and that its support is exactly the full
$(\alpha,\gamma)$-component through $u$.  Since $u\in X$ and
$v_1\notin X$, the predecessor of the vertex-node $v_1$ on $P$ is an
edge-node; call its edge identity $f_0$.  The preceding vertex-node lies
in $X$.  The two adjacencies in the incidence path and
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} show that the ends of $f_0$ are that
preceding tree vertex and $v_1$.  Thus
$f_0\in\partial_G(X)$ by \noderef{BoundaryEdges}.

Every edge-node of $P$ has color $\alpha$ or $\gamma$, again by
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}.  No $\gamma$-colored edge can be in
$\partial_G(X)$: $\gamma$ is missing at $u\in X$, and closedness, in the
sense of \noderef{ColoredVertexSetClosed}, forbids a boundary edge whose
color is missing at a tree vertex.  Consequently
\[
 \varphi(f_0)=\alpha,
 \qquad
 f_0\in\partial_{\varphi,\alpha}(X), \tag{2}
\]
where the second assertion uses \noderef{BoundaryColorEdges}.

There are at least three distinct identities in
$\partial_{\varphi,\alpha}(X)$ by
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumBoundaryFacts}, and
\noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainBoundaryEdges} says that the edge-node of
each of them lies on $P$.  An edge-node on this simple incidence path is
between its two endpoint vertex-nodes: it has precisely those incidence
neighbors by \noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} and the loopless endpoint
pair has two distinct entries.  If an $\alpha$-boundary edge-node other
than $f_0$ occurred before $v_1$, one of its two neighboring vertex-nodes
would already be outside $X$, contrary to the first-departure clause of
\noderef{ScheideDepartureReturn}.  Therefore $f_0$ is the only boundary
edge in the segment $uPv_1$, and at least two distinct
$\alpha$-boundary edge identities occur strictly after $v_1$.  In
particular $P$ continues past $v_1$, so $v_1$ is an internal vertex-node
of $P$.

Let $g_0$ be the edge identity at the edge-node immediately following
$v_1$.  The path is simple, so $g_0\ne f_0$.  Both identities are
incident with $v_1$, hence they are adjacent in the sense of
\noderef{AdjacentEdges}.  Properness from \noderef{KTriple}, expressed by
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring}, and (2) imply that $g_0$ cannot also have
color $\alpha$.  Its incidence-graph adjacency leaves only the other
possibility, so
\[
 \varphi(g_0)=\gamma. \tag{3}
\]
Properness also shows that $f_0$ is the unique incident $\alpha$-edge at
$v_1$ and $g_0$ is the unique incident $\gamma$-edge there.  Thus both
$\alpha$ and $\gamma$ belong to
\noderef{PresentColors}$(G,\varphi,v_1)$.  Since
\noderef{MissingColors} is the complement of the present-color set and
$\delta$ is missing at $v_1$, we have
\[
 \delta\ne\alpha,\qquad \delta\ne\gamma. \tag{4}
\]
The same definitions also give $\alpha\ne\gamma$: $\gamma$ is missing at
$u$, while $\alpha$ is present at $u$.  To see the latter assertion,
choose an $\alpha$-boundary edge using the at-least-three assertion above;
closedness of $X$ prevents its color from being missing at any vertex of
$X$, in particular at $u$.

Now let
\[
 C=\noderef{TwoColorComponent}(G,\varphi;\gamma,\delta,
       \operatorname{inl}v_1).
\]
This component has no vertex-node in $X$.  Indeed, $\gamma$ is missing at
$u\in X$ and $\delta$ is missing at $x\in X$.  Hence closedness of $X$
forbids every $\gamma$- or $\delta$-colored boundary identity.  If a
vertex-node of $X$ belonged to $C$, connectedness of the component and
$v_1\notin X$ would give a finite incidence walk from $v_1$ to $X$.
At the first vertex-node of this walk in $X$, the preceding edge-node
would be a $\gamma$- or $\delta$-colored boundary identity by
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} and \noderef{BoundaryEdges}, a
contradiction.  Moreover, no edge-node in $C$ is incident with a vertex
of $X$, since that incidence would put the vertex-node in the same
component.  Thus $C$ is disjoint not only from the vertex-nodes of $T$ but
also from every edge identity incident with $X$.

Define the first switched coloring by
\[
 \varphi_1=\noderef{TwoColorComponentSwap}
   (G,\varphi;\gamma,\delta,\operatorname{inl}v_1).
\]
By (4) and \noderef{TwoColorComponentSwapProper}, $\varphi_1$ is proper.
The swap definition changes only colored values $\gamma$ and $\delta$ on
edge-nodes in $C$; it fixes every uncolored value and sends every colored
value to a colored value.  Therefore the
\noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored} clause for $\varphi$ implies that
$e_0$ is still the unique uncolored edge under $\varphi_1$.

Because no edge-node of $C$ is incident with $X$, every edge incident
with any $w\in X$ has exactly the same value under $\varphi_1$ and
$\varphi$.  Unfolding \noderef{PresentColors} and then taking complements
with \noderef{MissingColors} gives
\[
 \operatorname{PresentColors}(G,\varphi_1,w)
   =\operatorname{PresentColors}(G,\varphi,w),
 \qquad
 \overline{\varphi_1}(w)=\overline\varphi(w)
 \quad(w\in X). \tag{5}
\]
In particular $\gamma$ remains missing at $u$.  Every displayed tree edge
has both ends in $X$ by the attachment clauses of
\noderef{TreeSequence}, so its value is unchanged.  The ordered vertices,
edge identities, distinctness, attachments, and first edge of the
sequence are independent of the coloring.  Hence it is the same
tree-sequence.  For each displayed edge after the first, its old
\noderef{TashkinovTree} witness consists of its unchanged color and an
earlier tree vertex missing that color; (5) preserves that missing-color
witness.  Thus $T$ is still a Tashkinov tree for
$(e_0,\varphi_1)$.

At $v_1$, the color $\delta$ was missing, while $g_0$ was the unique
incident $\gamma$-edge.  The node of $g_0$ lies in $C$, since it is
adjacent to the root vertex-node $v_1$.  The swap therefore changes
$g_0$ from $\gamma$ to $\delta$.  There was no incident
$\delta$-edge to be changed to $\gamma$, and uniqueness excluded any
second incident $\gamma$-edge.  Hence
\[
 \gamma\in\overline{\varphi_1}(v_1),
 \qquad \varphi_1(g_0)=\delta. \tag{6}
\]
The first switch fixes $f_0$ because its color $\alpha$ is distinct from
both switched colors.  It also fixes every edge in the earlier part of
$uPv_1$, because each such edge is incident with an earlier vertex in
$X$.

We next prove the support equality needed for the second switch, rather
than only a containment.  Let $D$ be the full
$(\alpha,\gamma)$-component of $\varphi_1$ through $u$, as defined by
\noderef{TwoColorComponent}.  Every edge-node of the prefix $uPv_1$ has
the same color under $\varphi_1$ as under $\varphi$, and consecutive
edges at each internal vertex alternate between $\alpha$ and $\gamma$:
they are distinct incident edges, each has one of those two colors by
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}, and properness forbids equal colors.
Thus the whole prefix lies in $D$.

Conversely, the prefix has no incidence by which $D$ can leave it.  At
$u$, $\gamma$ is missing by (5), while closedness and the defective
boundary edge show that $\alpha$ is present; properness makes the first
$\alpha$-edge unique.  At every internal vertex-node of the prefix, the
two displayed neighboring edge-nodes have the two different colors, and
properness permits no additional incident edge of either color.  At
$v_1$, (2) and (6) say that the entering identity $f_0$ is the unique
incident $\alpha$-edge and that $\gamma$ is missing.  Finally, every
edge-node of the prefix has exactly its two endpoint vertex-nodes as
incidence neighbors, and both are already in the prefix.  These endpoint
degree checks show that the prefix is a connected component of
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} for $\varphi_1$.  Since it contains $u$,
\[
 D=\operatorname{supp}(uPv_1). \tag{7}
\]

Define the twice-switched coloring
\[
 \varphi_2=\noderef{TwoColorComponentSwap}
   (G,\varphi_1;\alpha,\gamma,\operatorname{inl}u).
\]
The already proved inequality $\alpha\ne\gamma$ and
\noderef{TwoColorComponentSwapProper} show that $\varphi_2$ is proper.
As for the first switch, \noderef{TwoColorComponentSwap} fixes uncolored
values and never makes a colored value uncolored.  Since $e_0$ was the
unique uncolored edge for $\varphi_1$, it remains the unique uncolored
edge for $\varphi_2$.

We verify explicitly how this second switch affects the tree.  The
endpoint $u$ of $D$ has a unique incident $\alpha$-edge and no incident
$\gamma$-edge under $\varphi_1$, so its missing-color set changes only by
replacing $\gamma$ with $\alpha$:
\[
 \alpha\in\overline{\varphi_2}(u),\qquad
 \overline{\varphi_2}(u)
   =(\overline{\varphi_1}(u)\setminus\{\gamma\})\cup\{\alpha\}. \tag{8}
\]
Every other tree vertex on the prefix is internal to $D$: the only other
endpoint is the outside vertex $v_1$.  Such a tree vertex has exactly one
incident edge of each of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$, and the switch exchanges
those two identities.  Its present-color set, and hence its missing-color
set, is unchanged.  If a tree vertex is not on the prefix, no edge-node
of $D$ is incident with it, for such an incidence would put that
vertex-node in the component $D$.  Its missing-color set is unchanged as
well.  Thus (8) is the only change among tree-vertex missing-color sets.

No displayed tree-edge value changes in the second switch.  Indeed,
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumUnused} says that the defective color
$\alpha$ and the free color $\gamma$ are absent from the original used
set \noderef{TreeUsedColors}.  The first switch fixed every displayed
tree edge, so each displayed colored edge after $e_0$ still has a color
different from $\alpha$ and $\gamma$, and therefore its edge-node is not
in $D$.  The first displayed edge is $e_0$ and is uncolored, so it too is
fixed.  Consequently \noderef{TreeUsedColors} itself has the same value
for $T$ under all three colorings.

It follows clause by clause that $T$ is a Tashkinov tree for
$(e_0,\varphi_2)$.  Its \noderef{TreeSequence} data and all its
distinctness and attachment clauses are literally unchanged.  For a
displayed colored edge, both its identity and color are unchanged.  If an
old \noderef{TashkinovTree} witness vertex is not $u$, its entire missing
set is unchanged.  If the witness vertex is $u$, the witnessed edge color
is a member of \noderef{TreeUsedColors}, hence is neither $\alpha$ nor
$\gamma$; (8) therefore preserves membership of that witnessed color in
the missing set.  Every old Tashkinov witness survives.  This proves all
the old-edge clauses, not merely that the vertex set is unchanged.

It remains to construct the forbidden augmentation.  By the discussion
preceding (3), the prefix contains exactly the first
$\alpha$-boundary identity $f_0$, while at least two distinct identities
of $\partial_{\varphi,\alpha}(X)$ occur later on $P$.  Choose one of
these later identities and call it $f$.  The first switch only exchanges
$\gamma$ and $\delta$, both distinct from $\alpha$, so
$\varphi_1(f)=\alpha$.  By (7), the support of the second switch is
exactly the prefix, and the later edge-node of $f$ is not in that support.
Therefore
\[
 \varphi_2(f)=\alpha. \tag{9}
\]
The original membership
$f\in\partial_{\varphi,\alpha}(X)$ and
\noderef{BoundaryEdges} supply endpoints $a\in X$ and $b\notin X$ with
$G.\operatorname{ends}(f)=\{a,b\}$.  The identity $f$ is not a displayed
edge of $T$: $e_0$ is uncolored under $\varphi$, whereas $f$ has color
$\alpha$, and every later displayed edge with color $\alpha$ would put
$\alpha$ in \noderef{TreeUsedColors}, contrary to
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumUnused}.

Write the ordered data of the old tree as
\[
 T=(y_0,e_1,y_1,\ldots,e_n,y_n),\qquad e_1=e_0,
\]
and append the particular edge identity $f$ and its outside endpoint:
\[
 T^+=(y_0,e_1,y_1,\ldots,e_n,y_n,f,b).
\]
We check every \noderef{TreeSequence} clause.  The old vertices and edges
remain pairwise distinct.  The new vertex $b$ is distinct from every old
vertex because $b\notin X=V(T)$.  The new edge $f$ is distinct from every
old displayed edge by the preceding paragraph.  Every old attachment is
unchanged, while the new edge has ends $a$ and $b$, with
$a\in X$ an earlier displayed vertex.  The first edge is still $e_0$ and
the length is still positive.  Thus $T^+$ is a tree-sequence and
\[
 V(T^+)=X\cup\{b\},\qquad |V(T^+)|=|X|+1. \tag{10}
\]

All old edges of $T^+$ satisfy their Tashkinov clauses under $\varphi_2$
by the clause-by-clause verification above.  The new last edge $f$ has
color $\alpha$ by (9), and the earlier tree vertex $u$ is missing
$\alpha$ by (8).  Hence $u$ is the required witness for the new last-edge
clause in \noderef{TashkinovTree}.  Therefore $T^+$ is a Tashkinov tree
for $(e_0,\varphi_2)$.

The criticality, chromatic-index, and maximum-degree clauses of
\noderef{KTriple} depend only on $G$ and $k$, so they are inherited from
the original $k$-triple.  We proved that $\varphi_2$ is proper and that it
still leaves exactly $e_0$ uncolored.  Hence
$(G,e_0,\varphi_2)$ is again a $k$-triple.  Its chromatic-index clause
gives $k=\chi'(G)-1$, so $(e_0,\varphi_2,T^+)$ is among the choices in
\noderef{TashkinovOrder}.  Thus $|V(T^+)|\le t(G)$.  On the other hand,
\noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple} gives $|X|=t(G)$, and (10) gives
$|V(T^+)|=t(G)+1$, a contradiction.  This proves (1).

Finally unfold \noderef{ColoredVertexSetElementary}.  Two distinct
vertices of $X$ have disjoint missing-color sets by the elementariness
clause of \noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumOrder}.  Equation (1) says that
the missing-color set at $v_1$ is disjoint from the missing-color set at
every vertex of $X$, and
\noderef{ScheideDepartureReturn} gives $v_1\notin X$.  Hence every pair
of distinct vertices in $X\cup\{v_1\}$ has disjoint missing-color sets.
Therefore $X\cup\{v_1\}$ is elementary with respect to $\varphi$.
\end{proof}
