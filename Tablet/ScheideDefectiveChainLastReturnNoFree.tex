\begin{lemma}[Defective-chain structure, part (d)]
Let $(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be a globally maximum triple for $G$.  Let
$\alpha\in\mathcal D(V(T),\varphi)$ be defective, let
$\gamma\in\mathcal F(T,e_0,\varphi)$ be free and missing at a vertex
$u\in V(T)$, and let $z\notin V(T)$.  Let the supplied incidence walk $P$
from $u$ to $z$ be a full defective-chain path for
$(T,\varphi,\alpha,\gamma,u,z)$.  If the supplied indices $i_1<i_2$ and
vertices $v_1,v_2$ satisfy the first-departure/last-return predicate for
$(T,\varphi,\alpha,\gamma,u,z,P)$, then the last returning vertex $v_2$
misses no free color:
\[
 \overline\varphi(v_2)\cap\mathcal F(T,e_0,\varphi)=\varnothing.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $X=V(T)$, where the vertex set is the one defined in
\noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet}.  Unpack the supplied predicate
\noderef{ScheideDepartureReturn}.  It says that the vertex-node at $i_2$
is $v_2\in X$, that $i_2$ occurs strictly after the first outside
vertex-node, and that every vertex-node strictly after $i_2$ is outside
$X$.  Let $R=v_2Pz$ be the terminal incidence subwalk starting at that
occurrence of $v_2$.  Since $z\notin X$ by the full-path predicate
\noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainPath}, $R$ has an edge after $v_2$.

The bipartite definition \noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} makes the nodes
of $R$ alternate between multigraph vertices and individual edge
identities.  The first edge-node of $R$ is incident with $v_2$, and the
next vertex-node is outside $X$; by \noderef{Incident} and
\noderef{BoundaryEdges}, that edge identity is in $\partial_G(X)$.  If a
later edge-node of $R$ were a second boundary identity, its two neighboring
vertex-nodes on $R$ would be its two ends, again by
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} and \noderef{Incident}.  Exactly one of
those ends would then lie in $X$ by \noderef{BoundaryEdges}, contradicting
the last-return clause that every later vertex-node is outside $X$.
Thus
\[
 V(R)\cap X=\{v_2\},\qquad
 |E(R)\cap\partial_G(X)|=1.                                      \tag{1}
\]
This counts edge identities, so it remains valid when parallel edges have
the same endpoints.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that some color $\beta$ belongs both to
$\noderef{MissingColors}(G,\varphi,v_2)$ and to the free-color set
$\noderef{TreeFreeColors}(\varphi,T)$.  The first-departure index is
strictly before $i_2$, whereas $u$ is the initial vertex of $P$.  The path
clause of \noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainPath} makes the incidence-node
occurrences injective, so the later occurrence of $v_2$ cannot equal the
initial occurrence of $u$; hence $v_2\ne u$.  The maximum-order result
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumOrder} says that $X$ is elementary.  By the
definition \noderef{ColoredVertexSetElementary}, the missing-color sets
at the distinct vertices $u$ and $v_2$ are disjoint.  Since $\gamma$ is
missing at $u$ and $\beta$ is missing at $v_2$, this gives
$\beta\ne\gamma$.

We shall also use that $\alpha$ is present at every vertex of $X$.  The
defective-color hypothesis, interpreted through
\noderef{DefectiveColors}, and
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumBoundaryFacts} give at least three
$\alpha$-colored boundary identities.  On the other hand,
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumOrder} says that $X$ is closed.  The
definition \noderef{ColoredVertexSetClosed}, applied to any one of those
boundary identities, shows that $\alpha$ is missing at no vertex of $X$.
Because \noderef{MissingColors} is the complement of
\noderef{PresentColors}, $\alpha$ is present at every vertex of $X$.
In particular, $\alpha\ne\gamma$ because $\gamma$ is missing at $u$, and
$\alpha\ne\beta$ because $\beta$ is missing at $v_2$.

Both $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are missing somewhere on $X$: for $\beta$ this
is our assumption at $v_2$, and for $\gamma$ it is the hypothesis at $u$;
equivalently, both lie in the union described by
\noderef{TreeMissingColors}.  The missing-color clause of
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumBoundaryFacts}, which is the boundary
consequence of closedness in \noderef{ColoredVertexSetClosed}, therefore
gives
\[
 \noderef{BoundaryColorEdges}(G,\varphi,X,\beta)=\varnothing,
 \qquad
 \noderef{BoundaryColorEdges}(G,\varphi,X,\gamma)=\varnothing.   \tag{2}
\]
Here \noderef{BoundaryColorEdges} records the exact identities in
\noderef{BoundaryEdges}, not merely their endpoint pairs.

Define
\[
 \varphi_1=\noderef{OutsideTreeColorSwap}(T,\varphi,\beta,\gamma).
\]
By that definition, every edge identity incident with $X$ is fixed, while
on every other edge the two distinct colors $\beta,\gamma$ are
transposed and every other value, including the uncolored value, is
fixed.  Consequently every edge incident with every $x\in X$ keeps its
value.  The definition \noderef{PresentColors} then gives equality of the
present-color sets of $\varphi$ and $\varphi_1$ at every $x\in X$, and
\noderef{MissingColors} gives equality of the corresponding missing-color
sets.  Every boundary edge is incident with $X$, so all the sets in
\noderef{BoundaryColorEdges} are unchanged.  Hence the defective-color
set in \noderef{DefectiveColors} is unchanged.  Every displayed tree edge
is incident with $X$, so its value is unchanged; together with the
unchanged tree missing-color sets, the definitions
\noderef{TreeUsedColors}, \noderef{TreeMissingColors}, and
\noderef{TreeFreeColors} show that the used, tree-missing, and free-color
sets are also unchanged.

We verify properness of $\varphi_1$ directly.  The globally maximum
condition \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple} contains a
\noderef{KTriple}, whose \noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring} clause makes
$\varphi$ proper.  Let $e,f$ be distinct adjacent colored edge identities.
By \noderef{AdjacentEdges}, some vertex $w$ is incident with both in the
sense of \noderef{Incident}.  If both identities are incident with $X$,
both are fixed.  If neither is incident with $X$, the same transposition
of the distinct colors $\beta,\gamma$ is applied to both, so equality of
their new colors would imply equality of their old colors.  Finally,
suppose exactly one, say $e$, is incident with $X$.  The common vertex
$w$ must be outside $X$, since otherwise $f$ would also be incident with
$X$.  Thus $e$ has one end in $X$ and its common end $w$ outside $X$, so
$e\in\partial_G(X)$ by \noderef{BoundaryEdges}.  Equation (2) says that
its fixed color is neither $\beta$ nor $\gamma$.  A transposition of
$\beta,\gamma$ on $f$ can equal that fixed color only if $f$ already had
the same color, again contradicting properness of $\varphi$.  These cases
prove that $\varphi_1$ is proper.

The unique-uncolored clause is also preserved without any appeal to
maximality.  The \noderef{KTriple} contained in the globally maximum
triple gives \noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored}$(\varphi,e_0)$: the value on
$e_0$ is uncolored and every other edge identity has a colored value.
The definition of the outside swap fixes an uncolored value and sends a
colored value either to another colored value or to itself.  Hence
\[
 \noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored}(\varphi_1,e_0).                    \tag{3}
\]

We next prove that $R$, not merely some subpath containing it, is the
complete $(\alpha,\beta)$-component through $v_2$ under $\varphi_1$.
The first edge of $R$ is the unique boundary identity from (1).  It lies
on the old $(\alpha,\gamma)$-walk, so
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} says that its old color is $\alpha$ or
$\gamma$.  Equation (2) excludes $\gamma$ on a boundary edge; hence it
has color $\alpha$, and the outside swap fixes it.  Every later edge of
$R$ has both ends outside $X$ by (1).  Along the proper old two-color path,
successive edge identities have alternating colors $\alpha,\gamma$:
they are distinct adjacent edges by \noderef{AdjacentEdges} and
\noderef{Incident}, and equal colors are forbidden by
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring}.  The outside swap fixes $\alpha$ and
changes each such outside $\gamma$-edge to $\beta$.  Thus $R$ is an
$(\alpha,\beta)$-walk under $\varphi_1$.

There is no extension of this walk before $v_2$.  Because $i_2$ is after
the first departure, $v_2$ has a predecessor edge on $P$.  It is distinct
from the first edge of $R$ by the path clause in
\noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainPath}.  Both are incident with $v_2$ and
have colors in $\{\alpha,\gamma\}$ by
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph}; the first has color $\alpha$, so
properness forces the predecessor to have color $\gamma$.  It is incident
with $X$ and therefore remains $\gamma$ under $\varphi_1$.  Since
$\gamma\notin\{\alpha,\beta\}$, this predecessor is absent from the new
two-color graph.  Moreover, $\beta$ is still missing at $v_2$ and the
first edge of $R$ is the unique incident $\alpha$-edge by properness.
Thus the vertex-node $v_2$ has degree one in the new two-color component.

At every later internal vertex-node of $R$, its two incident edges on
$R$ have the two different colors $\alpha$ and $\beta$.  Properness of
$\varphi_1$ permits no second incident edge of either color, so no branch
can leave $R$ there.  At an edge-node, the bipartite construction
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} has only the two endpoint incidences,
both already used by $R$.

At the terminal vertex $z$, the original component has degree one.  Here
is the full justification.  Properness of $\varphi$ and
\noderef{TwoColorIncidenceDegreeAtMostTwo} give degree at most two at every
node of the old $(\alpha,\gamma)$-incidence graph.  At $u$, the color
$\gamma$ is missing and $\alpha$ is present, so the degree is one.  The
support equality in \noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainPath} identifies the
simple path $P$ with the whole finite connected set
\noderef{TwoColorComponent}$(G,\varphi,\alpha,\gamma,\operatorname{inl}u)$.
To see directly why this component is a path, start at the degree-one node
$u$ and follow its unique incident edge.  At every subsequent node there
is at most one edge other than the edge just used, so no branch can occur.
Finiteness forces the traversal either to stop or to revisit a node.  A
revisit would form a cycle; the first node at which the route from $u$
met that cycle would have the entering route edge and two cycle edges,
contrary to the degree-two bound (and $u$ itself cannot lie on the cycle
because it has degree one).  Hence the traversal stops at a second
degree-one node, and connectedness puts every component node on this one
path.  Since the full simple path $P$ starts at $u$ and ends at $z$, that
second endpoint is $z$.  Thus $z$ has degree one.

Any new $\alpha$- or $\beta$-edge incident with $z$ corresponds to an old
$\alpha$- or $\gamma$-edge there.  Indeed, an edge with both ends outside
$X$ is transformed by the $\beta,\gamma$ transposition, whereas a boundary
edge is fixed; in the boundary case (2) excludes both $\beta$ and
$\gamma$.  The old degree-one conclusion at $z$ therefore gives degree
one at $z$ in the new two-color graph.  We have now checked both endpoints
and every internal vertex- and edge-node: the support of $R$ is connected
and no incidence-graph edge leaves it.  By the definition
\noderef{TwoColorComponent},
\[
 \operatorname{supp}(R)=
 \noderef{TwoColorComponent}
   (G,\varphi_1,\alpha,\beta,\operatorname{inl}v_2).             \tag{4}
\]

Define the second coloring by switching this complete component:
\[
 \varphi_2=
 \noderef{TwoColorComponentSwap}
   (G,\varphi_1,\alpha,\beta,\operatorname{inl}v_2).
\]
We proved $\alpha\ne\beta$, and $\varphi_1$ is proper, so
\noderef{TwoColorComponentSwapProper} shows that $\varphi_2$ is proper.
The definition \noderef{TwoColorComponentSwap} again fixes an uncolored
value and maps every colored value to a colored value.  Applying this
observation edge by edge to (3) proves explicitly that
\[
 \noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored}(\varphi_2,e_0).                    \tag{5}
\]

We determine the missing-color change on $X$.  Equation (4) and (1) say
that the component meets $X$ only in the vertex-node $v_2$.  If a changed
edge were incident with a different $x\in X$, then the incidence
adjacency from \noderef{TwoColorIncidenceGraph} would put $x$ in the same
component, a contradiction.  At $v_2$, the component contains exactly its
one incident $\alpha$-edge and no $\beta$-edge, as proved above.  The
second swap changes that edge from $\alpha$ to $\beta$.  Therefore the
present-color set at $v_2$ replaces $\alpha$ by $\beta$, and, by the
complement definition \noderef{MissingColors}, its missing-color set
replaces $\beta$ by $\alpha$.  All other tree-vertex present and missing
sets are unchanged.  In particular,
\[
 \alpha\in\noderef{MissingColors}(G,\varphi_2,v_2),              \tag{6}
\]
and the only possible loss of an old missing-color fact on $X$ is the
loss of $\beta$ at $v_2$.

Every displayed tree-edge value is unchanged through both swaps.  The
first swap fixes it because every tree edge is incident with $X$.  For the
second swap, \noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumUnused} says that the defective
color $\alpha$ and the free color $\beta$ are both absent from the
displayed used-color set \noderef{TreeUsedColors}.  Thus no displayed
colored tree-edge node belongs to the component (4); the first displayed
edge is $e_0$, which is uncolored by (3), and so it does not belong either.

We now check every old Tashkinov witness rather than merely asserting
that the tree remains Tashkinov.  The globally maximum condition in
\noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple} supplies
\noderef{TashkinovTree}$(G,e_0,\varphi,T)$.  For each displayed edge after
the first, choose its old witness: its unchanged color $c$ was missing at
an earlier displayed vertex $w$.  The first swap preserves all such
missing sets.  Under the second swap, (6) shows that this witness can be
lost only if $w=v_2$ and $c=\beta$.  But $c$ is the color of a displayed
tree edge and so belongs to \noderef{TreeUsedColors}, whereas
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumUnused} says that $\beta$ does not.  This is
impossible.  Hence every old witness required by
\noderef{TashkinovTree} remains valid for $\varphi_2$.

It remains to append one exact boundary edge identity.  By
\noderef{ScheideGlobalMaximumBoundaryFacts}, there are at least three
original $\alpha$-colored identities in $\partial_G(X)$.  The equality in
\noderef{ScheideDefectiveChainBoundaryEdges} says that each of their
edge-nodes lies on $P$.  Equation (1) says that exactly one boundary
identity lies in $R$.  The first swap fixes every boundary edge, and the
second swap changes only edge-nodes in the complete component (4), namely
the support of $R$.  Therefore at least two of those original identities
lie outside $R$ and still have color $\alpha$ under $\varphi_2$.  Fix one
such identity $f$.

By \noderef{BoundaryEdges}, the two ends of $f$ can be named $x\in X$
and $y\notin X$.  Write the old data from \noderef{TreeSequence} as
\[
 T=(y_0,e_1,y_1,\ldots,e_n,y_n),\qquad e_1=e_0,
\]
and append the exact edge identity and outside endpoint:
\[
 T'=(y_0,e_1,y_1,\ldots,e_n,y_n,f,y).
\]
We check every tree-sequence clause.  Since $y\notin X$, it is different
from every old displayed vertex.  The identity $f$ is different from
every old displayed tree edge: the attachment clause in
\noderef{TreeSequence} gives both ends of each old tree edge in $X$,
whereas $f$ is a boundary edge with the end $y\notin X$.  The old vertex
and edge injectivity clauses persist.  Since $x\in X$, the definition
\noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet} supplies an earlier displayed occurrence
of $x$, which is the required attachment for $f$ and the new vertex $y$.
Every old attachment remains unchanged, and the first edge is still
$e_1=e_0$.  Thus $T'$ is a tree-sequence, its vertex set is
$X\cup\{y\}$, and
\[
 |V(T')|=|X|+1.                                                  \tag{7}
\]

All old Tashkinov witnesses for $T'$ were checked above.  The retained
edge $f$ has color $\alpha$ under $\varphi_2$, and (6) says that
$\alpha$ is missing at the earlier vertex $v_2\in X$.  Hence $v_2$ is
the witness required by \noderef{TashkinovTree} for the new last edge.
Therefore $T'$ is a Tashkinov tree for $(e_0,\varphi_2)$.

Finally we rebuild the same $k$-triple.  The criticality of $G$, the
equality $\chi'(G)=k+1$, and the maximum-degree inequality are the
coloring-independent clauses of the original \noderef{KTriple} contained
in \noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple}.  The remaining two clauses are exactly
the properness of $\varphi_2$ and (5).  Thus
$\noderef{KTriple}(G,k,e_0,\varphi_2)$ holds.  Its chromatic-index equality
makes $T'$ one of the trees whose order is bounded by
\noderef{TashkinovOrder}$(G)$, so
$|V(T')|\leq\noderef{TashkinovOrder}(G)$.  But the original
\noderef{GlobalMaximumTriple} says
$|X|=\noderef{TashkinovOrder}(G)$, contradicting (7).  The assumed
$\beta$ cannot exist, and the required intersection is empty.
\end{proof}
