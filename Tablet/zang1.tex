\begin{lemma}[Stable colorings preserve failure of strong closure]
Let $\varphi$ and $\pi$ be proper partial $k$-edge-colorings leaving exactly
the same tree edge $e_0$ uncolored.  Suppose that $|V(T)|$ is odd, that
$V(T)$ is closed but not strongly closed with respect to $\varphi$, and
that $\pi$ is $(T,C,\varphi)$-stable.  Then $V(T)$ is closed but not
strongly closed with respect to $\pi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $X=V(T)$, $M_\varphi=\overline\varphi(T)$, and
$M_\pi=\overline\pi(T)$.  Stability condition (ii) in
\noderef{StableColoring} gives
$\overline\pi(v)=\overline\varphi(v)$ for every $v\in X$.
By \noderef{TreeMissingColors}, $M_\pi$ and $M_\varphi$ are respectively
the unions of these vertexwise missing-color sets over the same finite set
$X$.  Taking the finite biunion of the displayed equality over all
$v\in X$ gives
\[
 M_\pi=\bigcup_{v\in X}\overline\pi(v)
      =\bigcup_{v\in X}\overline\varphi(v)=M_\varphi.
\]
Denote this common set by $M$.

We first prove closure under $\pi$.  Suppose that $f\in\partial_G(X)$ and
$\pi(f)=c$ for some $c\in M$.  The definition of the boundary in
\noderef{BoundaryEdges} says that exactly one end of $f$ lies in $X$.
Consequently $f$ has an end in $V(T)$ and is incident with $T$ in the
sense of \noderef{TreeIncidentEdge}.  Since
$c\in M=M_\varphi\subseteq M_\varphi\cup C$, the implication in the
alternate stability criterion \noderef{sc1}, applied on its $\pi(f)$ side,
gives $\varphi(f)=\pi(f)=c$.  On the other hand,
$c\in M_\varphi$ and \noderef{TreeMissingColors} supply a vertex
$v\in X$ at which $c$ is missing under $\varphi$.  The boundary membership
of $f$ and the equality $\varphi(f)=c$ now contradict closure under
$\varphi$ as defined in \noderef{ColoredVertexSetClosed}.  Hence no color
in $M=M_\pi$ occurs on a boundary edge under $\pi$, which is exactly that
$X$ is closed under $\pi$.

Let $t=|[k]\setminus M|$.  We next prove that every color
$c\in[k]\setminus M$ occurs on at least one boundary edge under
$\varphi$.  Fix such a $c$ and a vertex $v\in X$.  If $c$ were in
$\overline\varphi(v)$, then the union definition in
\noderef{TreeMissingColors} would put $c$ in $M$, a contradiction.  Thus
$c\notin\overline\varphi(v)$.  Since \noderef{MissingColors} defines the
missing set as the complement in $[k]$ of the present set, $c$ is present
at $v$.  By \noderef{PresentColors}, there is therefore an edge identity
$f_v$ incident with $v$ such that $\varphi(f_v)=c$.

This edge $f_v$ is unique.  Indeed, if distinct edges $f$ and $g$ were
both incident with $v$ and both had $\varphi$-color $c$, then they would be
adjacent by \noderef{AdjacentEdges}, contrary to properness as defined in
\noderef{IsProperEdgeColoring}.  Hence the number of incidences
$(v,f)$ with $v\in X$, $f$ incident with $v$, and $\varphi(f)=c$ is
exactly $|X|$, one for each $v\in X$.

Let $a_c$ be the number of $c$-colored edge identities whose two ends lie
in $X$, and let $b_c$ be the number of $c$-colored edge identities in
$\partial_G(X)$.  Incidence means endpoint membership by
\noderef{Incident}.  The endpoint-pair and loopless conditions in
\noderef{LooplessMultigraph}, together with the exactly-one-end definition
of \noderef{BoundaryEdges}, show that an internal $c$-edge contributes two
of the counted incidences, a boundary $c$-edge contributes one, and an
edge with neither end in $X$ contributes none.  Therefore
\[
                         |X|=2a_c+b_c.
\]
The left side is odd, so $b_c$ is odd and in particular $b_c>0$.
Thus each of the $t$ colors outside $M$ occurs on at least one boundary
edge under $\varphi$.

The first edge identity of $T$ is $e_0$ and has both of its ends among the
displayed vertices of $T$, by \noderef{TreeSequence}; those vertices form
$X$ by \noderef{TreeSequenceVertexSet}.  Thus both ends of $e_0$ lie in
$X$, whereas a boundary edge has exactly one end in $X$ by
\noderef{BoundaryEdges}.  It follows that $e_0\notin\partial_G(X)$.
Since each coloring leaves exactly $e_0$ uncolored,
\noderef{IsOneEdgeUncolored} now implies that every boundary edge is
colored under both $\varphi$ and $\pi$.

Because $X$ is closed but not strongly closed under $\varphi$, the second
clause of \noderef{ColoredVertexSetStronglyClosed} fails: two distinct
boundary edge identities have the same $\varphi$-color.  Closure under
$\varphi$, via \noderef{ColoredVertexSetClosed}, ensures that this repeated
color lies outside $M$ (use the union description in
\noderef{TreeMissingColors}).  Use one of the two repeated edges as the
representative of that repeated color, and choose one of the boundary edges
already obtained for each of the other colors outside $M$.  This gives $t$
representatives.  A partial
coloring is a map from edge identities to single coloring values by
\noderef{PartialEdgeColoring}, so representatives of different colors are
distinct; the other repeated edge is distinct from its first representative
and cannot be a representative of any other color.  Consequently
\[
                         |\partial_G(X)|\geq t+1.       \tag{1}
\]

The boundary edge set depends only on $G$ and $X$ by
\noderef{BoundaryEdges}.  Under $\pi$ every one of its edges is colored,
as proved above.  The closure of $X$ under $\pi$ says that none can receive
a color in $M=M_\pi$, so all boundary edges receive one of the same $t$
colors in $[k]\setminus M$.  Inequality (1) and the pigeonhole principle
therefore give two distinct boundary edge identities with the same
$\pi$-color.  The distinct-boundary-color clause in
\noderef{ColoredVertexSetStronglyClosed} fails under $\pi$.  Together with
the closure already proved, this shows that $X$ is closed but not strongly
closed under $\pi$, as required.
\end{proof}
